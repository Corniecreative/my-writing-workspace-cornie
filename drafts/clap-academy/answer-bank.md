# Clap Academy: Interview Answer Bank

**Role:** Business Development & Marketing Executive
**Interview:** 60–75 minutes. Questions about your experience, plus practical "what would you do" scenarios.

This guide gives you every question in full, so you don't need the original sheet next to you. For each one you'll find:

- **The question:** exactly as they wrote it
- **What they really want to know:** the question in plain words
- **How to open:** your first sentence, so you never freeze
- **What you could say:** a spoken answer in your own voice. Don't memorise it word for word; learn the flow.
- **Remember:** one or two quick tips

Anything in **[square brackets]** is a detail only you know. Fill it in before the interview.

---

## Before you start: how to speak in this interview

**1. You don't need to say "That's a good question."**
It's fine once or twice, but not every time. These openers sound more natural:

- "So the way I'd approach that is…"
- "I've actually done something similar, so let me tell you about that."
- "Let me walk you through it step by step."
- "There are really three parts to that…"
- "Can I give you a real example?"

**2. It's OK to pause.** Saying "Let me think about that for a second" sounds confident, not weak.

**3. For "Tell me about a time…" questions, tell a short story in four steps.** This is called **STAR**:

- **S**ituation: where you were
- **T**ask: what you needed to do
- **A**ction: what *you* did (most of your answer goes here)
- **R**esult: what happened, with a number if you have one

**4. Keep each answer to about 1.5–2 minutes.** Then finish clearly, for example:

- "…so that's how I'd approach it."
- "Does that answer what you were looking for?"

**5. Link back to Clap Academy whenever you can.** For example: "…and that's exactly what I'd want to do for your corporate programmes."

### Your three proof points (use them again and again)
1. **ICES Cake Expo Nigeria:** your talk brought in more than 15 enquiries and three paid workshop sign-ups.
2. **AI readiness webinar for bakers:** 403 people signed up and 398 attended.
3. **Results from data:** return on investment up 40% (Angel Analytics), customer acquisition up 10% and cost per customer down 24% (Seed Mentors), website traffic up 103% across 12 client accounts (Pallette).

### Your big advantage
You have **15+ years in business and marketing**, and you've worked in **both the UK and Nigeria**, Clap's two main markets. Mention it naturally when it fits.

## Section A: Finding and winning new clients

### Q1. "Walk us through how you would identify 20 organisations in the UK or Nigeria that could benefit from Clap Academy's corporate AI training programmes. What research tools and selection criteria would you use?"

**What they really want to know:** Do you know how to find the *right* companies, not just any companies?

**How to open:** "So I'd start by being clear on who a good client actually looks like, before I open any tools."

**What you could say:**
> "So I'd start by being clear on who a good client actually looks like, before I open any tools.
>
> For me, there are four things I'd look for. First, **size**: probably organisations with 200 staff or more, because they have enough people to justify a training programme. Second, **sector**: industries feeling pressure to adopt AI, like financial services, professional services, telecoms and the public sector. Third, a **trigger**, meaning a sign they're thinking about AI right now. That could be job adverts for data or AI roles, a new Chief Digital Officer, or a news story about a digital transformation plan. And fourth, whether we have **any connection** to them already, because a warm introduction always works better.
>
> For tools, I'd use LinkedIn Sales Navigator to filter by size, industry and job titles. In the UK, I'd check Companies House and industry news. In Nigeria, I'd look at business news like BusinessDay and Nairametrics, plus ministry and agency websites. I'd also use ChatGPT or Claude to quickly summarise an annual report into a one-page brief, which saves a lot of time.
>
> Then I'd put everything into the CRM and score each organisation, so the top 20 get personalised outreach first.
>
> I do a version of this at Aviura already. After exhibitions, I research prospective manufacturers and organise their details for targeted outreach. So it's a process I'm comfortable with."

**Remember:** The key word is *trigger*. It shows you think about timing, not just names on a list.

### Q2. "You identify a large organisation that could benefit from AI training. How would you find the right decision-maker, establish contact and secure a meeting?"

**What they really want to know:** Can you get past the front door and reach the person who says yes?

**How to open:** "In a big organisation there's usually more than one person involved, so I'd start by mapping who's who."

> "In a big organisation there's usually more than one person involved, so I'd start by mapping who's who.
>
> There's normally the person who **signs off the budget**: a CEO, COO or HR Director. Then there's someone who **cares most about the problem**, often the Head of Learning and Development, or a digital transformation lead. That person can become our champion inside the company. And then there's **procurement**, who handle the paperwork.
>
> I'd find these people on LinkedIn and the company website, and check whether anyone in our network knows them, because a warm introduction is always better than a cold message.
>
> To make contact, I wouldn't jump straight to selling. I'd engage with their content on LinkedIn first, then send a short, personal email that mentions something specific about their organisation. And I'd ask for something small, like a 20-minute call, rather than a big meeting. Something like, 'Would it be useful to hear how similar organisations are approaching their first 90 days with AI?'
>
> Then I'd follow up a few times, across email, LinkedIn and phone, and keep track of it all in the CRM."

**Remember:** "Ask for something small" is a strong point. Small asks get more yeses.

### Q5. "Suppose you contacted 30 organisations and received only two responses. How would you diagnose the problem and improve your approach?"

**What they really want to know:** When things don't work, do you give up, or do you investigate and fix them?

**How to open:** "First, I wouldn't panic. A low response rate tells you something, so I'd treat it like a puzzle."

**What you could say:**
> "First, I wouldn't panic. A low response rate tells you something, so I'd treat it like a puzzle.
>
> I'd check four things, in order. One, the **list**: were these the right organisations, and was I contacting the right person? Two, the **message**: was it about them, or was it all about us? Did I mention something specific to their organisation? Three, the **channel**: did I only use email? Maybe a LinkedIn message or a phone call would work better. And four, the **follow-up**: did I only contact them once? Most replies come after the second or third touch.
>
> Then I'd change **one thing at a time** and test it, so I know what actually made the difference. I've done this kind of testing before. At Seed Mentors I ran A/B tests on campaigns, and that helped increase engagement by 12% and customer acquisition by 10%."

**Remember:** "Change one thing at a time" shows you're methodical.

## Section E: Practical scenarios

These are "what would you do" questions. Walk them through your thinking step by step. There's no single right answer; they want to see how you think.

### Scenario 1 (Q21). UK financial services company

**The question:** "A UK financial services organisation has 500 employees and is exploring AI adoption but has no structured workforce training programme. How would you research this organisation, identify its likely training needs and approach the relevant decision-maker? What would your first email say, and how would you follow up?"

**What they really want to know:** Show us exactly how you'd go from zero to a meeting.

**How to open:** "I'd do this in three steps: research, figure out their needs, then reach out."

**What you could say:**
> "I'd do this in three steps: research, figure out their needs, then reach out.
>
> **Research:** I'd look at their website, annual report, recent news, the jobs they're advertising, and LinkedIn to see who leads HR, Learning and Development, data and operations.
>
> **Their likely needs:** in financial services, the big worry with AI is risk: data protection and regulation. So they'd probably need training on using AI safely, practical training for staff to save time on everyday tasks, and a session for leaders so they understand what's possible.
>
> **Approach:** I'd target the Head of L&D or HR Director as my main contact. My first email would be short and specific. Something like:
>
> *'Hi Sarah, I noticed you're currently hiring for data roles. Many financial services teams we speak to have AI tools but no structured way to help staff use them safely. Clap Academy runs practical AI programmes, from leadership briefings to hands-on workshops, built around your regulatory needs. Would a 20-minute call be useful to share how similar firms are approaching it?'*
>
> **Follow-up:** about four days later, I'd connect on LinkedIn. A week after, I'd send something useful, like a short guide or article. Then a phone call. And if there's still no reply, a polite final email, so the door stays open."

**Remember:** Practise saying the email out loud. It should sound natural, not read out.

### Scenario 2 (Q22). Nigerian federal ministry

**The question:** "Clap Academy wants to approach a Nigerian federal ministry about an executive AI readiness and digital transformation programme. How would you identify the relevant stakeholders, prepare an organisational brief, establish contact and work towards a meeting or formal training proposal? How would you handle procurement requirements?"

**What they really want to know:** Do you understand how government works in Nigeria?

**How to open:** "With a federal ministry, the first thing I'd remember is that relationships and protocol matter just as much as the proposal."

**What you could say:**
> "With a federal ministry, the first thing I'd remember is that relationships and protocol matter just as much as the proposal.
>
> **Stakeholders:** I'd identify the Minister's office, the Permanent Secretary, the Director of Human Resources or Training, the director responsible for ICT or digital, and the procurement unit.
>
> **Organisational brief:** I'd put together a short document on the ministry: what it's responsible for, its current digital projects, its leadership, recent news, and how an AI readiness programme would support its goals, especially Nigeria's national digital agenda.
>
> **Making contact:** I'd start with a formal letter, ideally supported by an introduction from someone they know and trust: a professional network, alumni or diaspora contacts. Then I'd request a meeting and be patient, because these things take time.
>
> **Procurement:** I'd ask the procurement unit early what they need, rather than guessing. That usually means vendor registration and compliance documents. I'd also prepare a capability statement, examples of past work like the London programme for senior officials, and a clear, costed proposal that fits their budget timing."

**Remember:** "Ask procurement early rather than guessing" shows maturity.

### Scenario 3 (Q23). The client who went quiet

**The question:** "A corporate client has expressed interest in training 25 employees but has not responded for three weeks. What follow-up strategy would you implement to revive the opportunity without damaging the relationship?"

**What they really want to know:** Can you chase without annoying people?

**How to open:** "Three weeks of silence doesn't mean no. It usually just means they're busy."

**What you could say:**
> "Three weeks of silence doesn't mean no. It usually just means they're busy, so I'd follow up in a way that adds value each time.
>
> First, instead of 'just checking in', I'd send something **useful**, like a short case study from a similar organisation.
>
> If there's still nothing, I'd try a **different channel**: a phone call or a LinkedIn message.
>
> Next, I'd make it **easier to say yes**. Maybe offer a smaller first step, like a pilot session for a few people or a free 30-minute readiness check.
>
> I might also see if there's **another person** in the organisation involved in the decision.
>
> And finally, a polite message like, 'I don't want to keep filling your inbox. Should I close this for now, or is it still something you'd like to explore?' That often gets a reply, because it's honest and respectful.
>
> I'd record every step in the CRM so nothing gets forgotten."

**Remember:** The "should I close this for now?" email works really well. Mention it confidently.

### Scenario 4 (Q24). The London success story

**The question:** "Clap Academy has just completed a high-level executive training programme in London for senior government officials. How would you use the training experience to strengthen our corporate image, produce professional content, generate referrals and approach similar organisations?"

**What they really want to know:** How would you make the most of our biggest win?

**How to open:** "This is a fantastic story to have. Training senior government officials instantly builds credibility."

**What you could say:**
> "This is a fantastic story to have. Training senior government officials instantly builds credibility.
>
> The very first thing I'd do is **get the right permissions**, because with government officials you have to be careful about what's shared and how.
>
> Once that's in place, I'd create a **professional announcement** for LinkedIn and the website, a **photo story**, a **short highlight video**, and one or two **testimonial clips** if officials are happy to give them. I'd also write a **case study** our team can send to prospects.
>
> For **referrals**, I'd follow up with the delegates personally, thank them, and ask if there are other departments or agencies that would benefit.
>
> And for **new organisations**, it becomes a really strong opening line: 'We recently delivered an executive AI programme for senior government officials in London.' That opens doors with other ministries, agencies, and large organisations too."

**Remember:** Lead with permissions. It shows you understand how sensitive government work is.

### Scenario 5 (Q25). Your first 90 days

**The question:** "You join Clap Academy next month. What would your first 30, 60 and 90 days look like? What measurable results would you expect to deliver?"

**What they really want to know:** Do you have a clear plan, and will you deliver results?

**How to open:** "I'd think of it as three stages: learn and set up, start reaching out, then start converting."

**What you could say:**
> "I'd think of it as three stages: learn and set up, start reaching out, then start converting. And I'd want to agree the exact targets with you, but here's roughly how I see it.
>
> **First 30 days, learning and setting up.** I'd get to know all your programmes, pricing and past clients. I'd define exactly who our ideal clients are in the UK and Nigeria, build a list of at least 100 researched prospects in the CRM, and create the key materials: a capability statement, a case study and outreach templates. I'd also set up a simple weekly report so you can see progress.
>
> **Days 31 to 60, reaching out.** I'd aim for around 150 personalised contacts across email, LinkedIn and phone, and book at least 10 discovery calls. I'd also have a regular LinkedIn content plan running and record our first executive video or podcast.
>
> **Days 61 to 90, converting.** By then I'd hope to have four to six proposals submitted, and one or two new clients or pilot programmes signed. I'd also start a referral process with existing clients, and review what's working so we can set targets for the next quarter."

**Remember:** Saying "I'd want to agree the targets with you" shows respect for the CEO while still giving real numbers.

## Questions to ask them at the end

Always ask two or three. Pick from these:

- "Which of your programmes is most popular right now, and which would you most like to grow?"
- "Over the next six months, would you like the focus to be more on the UK or on Nigeria?"
- "What would a really successful first 90 days look like to you?"
- "Which CRM do you use at the moment?"
- "How do you see the business development work being shared between yourself and this role?"

**How to close the interview:**
> "Thank you so much for your time today. I've really enjoyed learning more about Clap Academy, and I'm genuinely excited about the role. Bringing together business development, marketing and AI across the UK and Nigeria is exactly what I'd love to be doing."
